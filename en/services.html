What gets built from it

Seven applications plating shops need again and again

Each of them runs on data your lines already write today — controller logs, sensor values, coating thickness measurements, backup files. Which one pays off first for you is decided by the findings.

The defect you cannot see on the part

Some patterns cause a defect that only shows up at your customer — and then triggers a recall. They cannot be spotted through the line's normal error messages. The analysis reports them the same day, so the batch can be traced and checked before it ships.

Batch documentation in minutes

Coating thicknesses, currents, bath values — your lines record all of this anyway, just not retrievable at the push of a button. When an automotive customer asks, the documentation for a batch is ready in minutes instead of after hours of searching.

Early warnings that reach the right person

Baths drifting out of spec and breakdowns happen at night and on weekends. An application on your server collects the data every few minutes and reports anything unusual to the on-call staff by email or text message — with a plain-language note on what came together and what to check.

Fewer alarms, but the right ones

Many controllers hold tens of thousands of warnings. Anyone who gets five of them every minute clicks them away — including the two a day that matter. The alarms are analyzed and sorted with you: the important ones up front, the rest moved to the background in a traceable way.

Maintenance when it is needed, not by the calendar

Like with a car: fixed intervals where they fit — and where production fluctuates, triggered by batches run, square feet plated or flow volume. These values are usually already available. Shops noticeably cut maintenance effort this way, because maintenance goes where it is needed.

The probe that reads wrong

Crusted, out of calibration, damaged — the probe does not report itself as faulty, it keeps delivering values. The analysis names which measuring points should be checked first today, without a single additional sensor. How this is detected.

Proof that your improvements work

A shop decides to sand the contacts regularly. Whether it happens and whether it works is recorded in the same logs — so far, nobody looks. From them come monthly metrics for every improvement project, for management at a glance.

Maintenance plans, checklists and safety routines often need no new software at all — the Microsoft tools you already have can handle them, including entries from a phone. What is missing is rarely the program, but the setup behind it.

The rest of the portfolio

What else you can order

Each of these is ordered separately, and none of them requires you to take the next step. Most of these topics only come up out of the Site Assessment anyway.

  • Implementation and project support — an area of potential becomes a project: technical leadership through to the solution, tasks and deadlines kept in view, a status report for management every two weeks. That takes the load off your people exactly where internal projects otherwise stall.
  • Preparing requests for proposal — when sensors, automation or an IT service provider is needed: requirements written up, quotes made comparable. You place the order and do the negotiating.
  • Data map — which of your systems record what, what is kept twice, where there is a gap.
  • Making knowledge accessible — work instructions, bath make-up instructions and technical literature become searchable: anyone trying to narrow down a fault asks in their own words and gets the relevant passage from your own documents. Runs on your server or in a European data space — not on a personal account. Helps where experience walks out the door with individual people.
  • Target curve instead of spot checks — for sites with dense data: from parts throughput, ampere-hours and bath age, the expected concentration curve of a bath is calculated and compared with the lab analysis. If it deviates, an alert goes out before the batch shows a problem.
  • Ongoing operation — the analysis keeps running, billed per monitored line, on your hardware and with no cloud. Model maintenance is included, so detection does not drift over time.

Where the limits are

Your contact is a master electroplater (Austrian master craftsman qualification) and materials physicist — you do not have to explain your process to anyone, but you also do not get a general digitalization consultant. Other trades are named and referred on, not coordinated. You will not be sold any hardware, and no commission is earned on any purchase. And you do not need a data platform pre-project as a prerequisite: the proof runs on the data you already have today.

Three things are deliberately not part of the offer. An analysis is not the first step: where dosing is still being adjusted by hand, the control loop needs to be closed first — that removes most of the variation, and only then does monitoring pay off. No data platform is built. A data warehouse quickly costs six figures to set up and keeps costing every year after that; what the small solution for the two or three most expensive baths costs is set next to it — and it usually wins. And corrective actions are technical, not organizational: a different parameter in the recipe, more flow in the rinse, longer-lasting sensors — instead of one more check that someone has to do on top of everything else.

And if you would rather enable your own people than order an analysis: that is the Data Workshop (in German) — the same process, except your own skilled staff build it themselves.

What people ask first

Eight questions, eight answers

How much of our time does this take? We have no one to spare.

On the most recent project, the customer team spent less than twenty hours in total over three months: twenty minutes of the morning meeting every month to month and a half — process engineering, maintenance, quality, production and management at one table.

Plus one person on your side as the point of contact, one to two hours a week, mainly to check whether the analyses are technically correct. The development is done by someone who can read the data himself — which keeps your effort small.

Do we have to get our data in order first?

No. The work is done on the data you have today — and almost everywhere it is a mix: logs from the line controller, sensor values, readings from the X-ray thickness gauge, orders in the ERP system. Much of it is already sitting in backup files without anyone using it.

The opposite approach — first merging everything into one big system — is the expensive one. In the automotive industry, that produced platforms nobody could operate afterward. Where something is really missing later on, it is named: usually one or two additional exports from the controller.

Does this interfere with our line controls?

No. The analysis uses what the controller already puts out — the controller is not changed and not operated remotely. If additional exports are needed, your line's programmer sets them up; the effort for that is manageable.

No new sensors and no labels on the parts are needed. The matching runs on what your line already records.

Where does the software run, and who operates it?

At your site — on a server or a virtual machine in your building. The application collects the data on its own, analyzes it and reports when something looks unusual. In normal operation, nobody operates it.

Opening an analysis does not take an engineer: it is designed for the people who work at the line. One hour of training is enough.

What happens to our data?

The finished system runs in your building — no cloud, no subscription. For the first demo you provide an extract voluntarily: only what you release, with encrypted handover and a confidentiality agreement. Your readings are analyzed exclusively on our own computers. No AI service sees them.

Formulations and process parameters are not needed to find anomalies. What a tool is allowed to see is agreed in writing beforehand — which frameworks stand behind this.

What if a probe is reading wrong?

A crusted or out-of-calibration probe does not report itself as faulty. It keeps delivering values — just the wrong ones, and the controller accepts them without question. It often only becomes noticeable through quality, which means late.

It can be spotted earlier: there are measurements from different sources that normally move in the same direction — parts throughput and the conductivity after the rinse, for example. If they drift apart, the analysis flags it and names the most unusual measuring points of the day. That way your people know where a check measurement or a calibration is due — without a single additional sensor.

Does this replace our skilled staff?

No — and monitoring that promised this would not hold up in a plating shop. Crusting, mechanical damage and the actual condition of a line show up when someone goes and looks, not in a series of numbers.

What the analysis takes off your hands is the searching: it tells you which spot and which time window to look at first. The judgment stays with your process engineering and maintenance teams — and they get back exactly the time that goes into troubleshooting today.

How can we tell whether a check actually took place at the line?

Through two separate tracks that are compared against each other: the line's values on one side, the manual check measurement on the other — recorded digitally by phone right at the line, instead of on a paper list.

If the difference exceeds a threshold agreed with you, an alert is raised. That proves two things at once: that the measurement was taken — and that the sensors arrive at the same result. For audits, this documents what would otherwise just be checked off.
